# Tabu (Israeli land registry) explained

The **Tabu** (Hebrew: טאבו) is Israel's central land registry - the official record of who owns every piece of real estate in the country. Pulling a "nesach tabu" (registry extract) is the **single most important step** before signing any purchase contract, because it tells you whether the seller actually has the right to sell what they're trying to sell.

The name "tabu" comes from the Ottoman-era Turkish *tapu* (literally "deed"). The system has been continuously maintained since 1858 and now lives in a Tax Authority database accessible to anyone.

## What the tabu tells you

A nesach tabu for a residential apartment shows:

1. **Block (גוש) and parcel (חלקה)** - the unique geographic identifier
2. **Sub-parcel (תת חלקה)** - the specific apartment within a building
3. **Registered owner(s)** - name, ID number, share owned (e.g. 1/1 for sole, 1/2 for spouses)
4. **Description** - the area in square meters, number of rooms
5. **Common areas** - entry, stairwell, basement storage, parking spots associated with the unit
6. **Encumbrances** - any liens, mortgages, easements, or third-party claims
7. **Restrictions** - building rights or future obligations (e.g. "subject to Tama 38 deal")
8. **History** - the chain of previous owners going back to the building's first registration

## Why you must pull it

Three things can go catastrophically wrong if you don't read the tabu before buying:

### 1. The seller doesn't actually own it (or only owns part)

You'd be amazed how often this happens. Common scenarios:

- The "owner" inherited and never finished the probate registration - siblings are co-owners
- The apartment was bought during a marriage, divorce is pending, and ownership is contested
- The seller is the deceased's son acting "on behalf of" the family, but no probate has happened

Your money goes to someone with no legal authority to transfer the property. Years of litigation follow.

### 2. There's a mortgage you didn't know about

The tabu shows the bank's mortgage charge. If the seller's bank is owed ₪1.5M and you pay them ₪2M, **the bank must release the mortgage before the title transfers to you**. Your lawyer handles this through escrow-style payment instructions, but only if they knew about the mortgage. If the lawyer never pulled the tabu, you could find yourself with title still encumbered.

### 3. There's a planning restriction or future obligation

Some apartments come with future commitments - "the buyer agrees to participate in a future Tama 38 renovation," "right of first refusal granted to neighbor," "easement allowing access to roof". The tabu shows these. If you don't read it, you might be agreeing to obligations you didn't know existed.

## How to pull a tabu

You can do it yourself online - no lawyer needed, takes 5 minutes:

1. Go to **https://www.gov.il/he/service/registration_and_information_about_real_estate**
2. Select "**רישום מקרקעין**" → "**הזמנת נסח טאבו**"
3. Enter the **gush + chelka** (block + parcel). Don't know it? You can look up by address.
4. Pay ~₪16 by credit card
5. Receive a PDF by email within minutes

The seller's listing agent should also provide one - but **don't rely on the agent's copy**. Pull your own to make sure it's current (tabus update in real-time). A copy that's 6 months old won't reflect a mortgage that was registered last month.

## Reading a nesach tabu

The document is in Hebrew (English nesachim are available but rare). Key sections:

**רישום הבעלות** (Ownership registration) - list of current owners, their ID numbers, and their share. Total shares must add to 1/1.

**שעבודים והערות** (Liens and notes) - any mortgages registered, with the bank name and amount. Each entry has a date and a registration number.

**הערות** (General notes) - easements, rights of way, restrictions. Read carefully.

**היסטוריה** (History) - previous owners. A "clean" chain means each transfer was properly registered. Gaps or unusual transactions (e.g. court orders) should prompt your lawyer to investigate.

## Common edge cases

### "The building is in regulation (חוקת בית משותף)"

Most apartment buildings in Israel are registered as a **בית משותף** (commonhold) - each apartment has its own sub-parcel in the tabu. Buying is straightforward.

### "The building isn't in regulation"

Some older buildings have never been formally split. The seller's "ownership" is recorded as a percentage of the whole building (e.g. 12.5% of gush 6213, chelka 47). This is harder to deal with - your lawyer needs to verify that the percentage corresponds to the specific apartment you're buying, and either complete the regulation (long process) or accept the ambiguity.

### "Minhal Mekarkei Israel land lease" (חכירה מנהל מקרקעי ישראל)

Roughly 90% of Israeli land is owned by the state and **leased**, not sold, to private owners. The tabu shows you as the **lessee**, not the freeholder. Leases are usually 49 or 98 years, automatically renewable, and treated by banks and the market as functionally identical to freehold. Don't be alarmed - this is normal.

If the building is **freehold** (בעלות פרטית), the tabu will say so explicitly. Slightly higher resale value but rare in the Tel Aviv area.

### "The apartment isn't in the tabu"

If a building is so new that the tabu hasn't been updated yet (typical for construction within the past 24 months), you'll see the apartment only at the **kablan's contract level** until registration completes. The developer's lawyer handles registration; until then your purchase is a contractual right, not a registered title. This is normal but adds risk - the kablan could in theory go bankrupt before registration.

## What about the new "Tabu Online" system?

The Tax Authority has been rolling out **eTabu** since 2022 - full digital workflows for buying, mortgaging, and selling. As of 2026, most cities are supported. The nesach you pull online is legally identical to the old paper one.
