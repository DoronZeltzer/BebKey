# Israeli mortgage (mashkanta) explained

A *mashkanta* is an Israeli mortgage. It's the loan that lets you buy property when you don't have all the cash. The Israeli system is more rigid than the US or UK - fewer products, stricter LTV caps, mandatory mix of interest-rate types - but the structure is well-defined and once you understand it you can shop confidently.

## How much can you borrow?

The Bank of Israel sets a hard cap on the loan-to-value (LTV) ratio depending on your buyer type:

- **First home (primary residence)** - up to **75%** LTV
- **Upgrader** (selling your previous home to buy this one) - up to **70%** LTV
- **Investor** (second or third Israeli property) - up to **50%** LTV

If you're financing a ₪2M apartment as a first-time buyer, your maximum loan is ₪1.5M. You need ₪500,000 in your bank account plus closing costs.

The bank also caps your **monthly payment as a percentage of net income**. The rule of thumb is the payment can't exceed **40% of your net income**, sometimes 35%. So if your household nets ₪25,000/month, your maximum monthly mortgage payment is ₪10,000.

## The mandatory track mix (תמהיל)

Israeli mortgages are almost always **split across multiple tracks** (משולש מסלולים). This is regulated, not optional. The Bank of Israel currently requires that:

- **At least one third** of the loan be in **fixed-interest tracks** - predictable monthly payments, no surprises
- **At most one third** be in **variable Prime tracks** - adjusts when the Bank of Israel changes the prime rate
- The rest can be in **inflation-linked** or **inflation-protected** tracks

The most common tracks:

| Track | Interest | Inflation-linked? | Typical % of loan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed, not-linked (קל"צ)** | Fixed for full term | No | 30-50% |
| **Fixed, linked (ק"צ)** | Fixed but adjusts to CPI | Yes | 10-30% |
| **Prime** | Bank of Israel's prime rate - margin | No | 30-33% |
| **Variable not-linked** | Resets every 5 years | No | 0-20% |
| **Variable linked** | Resets + CPI-adjusted | Yes | 0-20% |

The right mix depends on inflation expectations, your risk tolerance, and how long you plan to hold the loan. Most first-time buyers are advised to **maximize the fixed not-linked portion** (predictable) and **minimize linked tracks** (risky if Israel sees high inflation).

## How rates work

Israeli mortgage rates are quoted as **annual interest**. The Prime rate is set by the Bank of Israel - as of 2026 it's around 4.5-6%. The fixed-not-linked rate is typically Prime **plus** 0-1% spread. So a 30-year fixed loan might be 5-7% annual.

The **monthly payment** is calculated standard-amortization-style: principal + interest, fixed each month for the not-linked tracks, adjusting for the linked tracks.

> **Tip:** Always ask the bank for a **"luach silukin"** (amortization table) for each track. This shows month-by-month how much of each payment goes to interest vs principal.

## How banks evaluate you

The bank wants three things to be true:

1. **You have stable income.** Salaried employees show 3 months of pay slips. Self-employed show 2 years of tax returns. Mixed sources are accepted but treated conservatively.

2. **Your debt-to-income ratio is acceptable.** Total debt service (this mortgage + all other loans + credit cards) under 40% of net income.

3. **The property is worth what you're paying.** The bank does its own appraisal (שמאות). If their appraisal is lower than your purchase price, the loan amount drops accordingly and you cover the gap from your own pocket.

## How to negotiate

Get **three written offers** from different banks before you sign anything. The big four in Israel - **Bank Hapoalim, Bank Leumi, Mizrahi-Tefahot, Discount** - will compete on rates if they know you're shopping.

What's negotiable:

- **The spread above Prime** (banks publish a sticker spread but discount 0.3-0.7% for good borrowers)
- **The fixed-not-linked rate** (smaller room - usually ±0.2%)
- **Setup fees** (often waivable)
- **Early-payoff penalty** (the *kanas pireion*) - if you might refinance, ask for a reduced penalty

A 0.5% rate improvement on a ₪1.5M 30-year loan saves you **roughly ₪150,000 over the life of the loan**. It's worth the friction of shopping three banks.

## Olim special programs

If you made aliyah within the past 7 years, you may qualify for:

- **Misrad HaKlita (Ministry of Aliyah) mortgage** - up to ₪139,000 (varies by family size and location) at favorable terms
- **Higher LTV** - some banks treat olim's first Israeli home like a primary residence even if they own property abroad
- **Special non-resident programs** for buyers still living abroad

## The pre-approval (אישור עקרוני)

Before you start touring apartments, get a **pre-approval** from at least one bank. The bank reviews your income, debts, and equity, then commits in writing to a maximum loan amount, **valid for 6-12 months**.

This costs ~₪500-₪1,500 in fees but it:

- Gives you a hard budget number
- Lets you make offers credibly
- Speeds up the closing (the bank has already done the borrower review; only the property appraisal remains)

## After you sign the purchase contract

The bank does its own property appraisal (1-2 weeks), then formally approves the loan, then registers a **mortgage charge** at the **Tabu** (land registry) which prevents the seller from selling the same property twice.

Funds are transferred to the seller's lawyer at the agreed milestone, usually the key handover.

## Refinancing

Israeli mortgages can be refinanced - but every track has an **early-payoff penalty** (קנס פירעון) that depends on the rate differential. If interest rates have fallen, the penalty can be substantial. If they've risen, refinancing is essentially free.

Refinancing typically makes sense when:

- Rates have dropped more than 0.75% since you took the loan
- You're more than 5 years into the loan (less interest left to save)
- You're switching from a high-margin Prime track to a lower-spread one

A **mortgage advisor** (יועץ משכנתאות) is paid 0.5-1% of the loan amount and can negotiate better rates than you'll get alone, especially on refinancing.
